Reverse Osmosis Maintenance Schedule: When to Change Every Filter (2026)

Updated September 2026·10 min read

Reverse osmosis systems are fairly low-maintenance — for most home systems the routine comes down to one thing: changing the filters on schedule. Skip it, and you can expect slow flow, worse-tasting water, and eventually a damaged membrane. This guide covers what each stage does, example replacement intervals taken from makers’ listings, how to tell when it’s time, and what drives the cost.

The RO Maintenance Schedule (At a Glance)

Your system’s manual sets the schedule, and lifespans vary with your water quality and how much water you use. The intervals below are examples from specific makers’ listings, to show the typical pattern:

Stage What it does Example interval (per the maker’s listing)
Sediment pre-filter Catches sand, rust, dirt; protects everything downstream 6 months for iSpring’s RO500AK pre-filter (FCF500); follow your manual
Carbon pre-filter(s) Removes chlorine and taste; protects the membrane from chlorine damage Usually changed with the sediment filter; iSpring sells 6-month (F4AK) to 3-year (F28K75) sets for the RCC7AK
RO membrane The heart of the system; rejects dissolved contaminants 24 months for iSpring’s MRO500 and for the Waterdrop X8-WAN’s RO filter
Post-carbon “polish” filter Final taste refinement before the faucet 12 months for iSpring’s RO500AK post-filter (FCA500)
Alkaline / remineralization stage (if equipped) Adds minerals back for taste 9–12 months or 1,100 gallons for the Waterdrop MNR35 inline filter
UV lamp (if equipped) Disinfection stage, mainly for well water Per your system’s manual

The pattern: pre-filters most often, the membrane least often. Some systems have filter-life indicators (the iSpring RO500AK has filter-change lights and a buzzer, and Waterdrop’s G3P600 faucet shows filter life, per their listings); if yours doesn’t, mark the dates on your calendar.

Why the Schedule Matters (What Happens If You Skip It)

Each stage protects the next, so neglecting filters has knock-on effects:

  • Clogged pre-filters restrict flow, so your tank refills slowly and you get a trickle at the faucet. They also stop protecting the membrane.
  • Exhausted carbon pre-filters let chlorine reach the RO membrane — and chlorine permanently damages the membrane on most systems. This is the big one: skip your carbon filters and you can ruin the membrane early.
  • An old membrane slowly loses rejection, so more contaminants pass through. You won’t taste it happening — which is why testing (below) matters.
  • An old post-filter lets the water pick up a stale taste on its way to the faucet.

In short, the pre-filters protect the membrane, which is usually the priciest part to replace. Staying on schedule is what keeps an RO system working as designed.

How to Know It’s Time (Beyond the Calendar)

Three signs your filters or membrane need attention:

  1. Slower flow / longer tank refill. The most common signal that pre-filters are clogging.
  2. Taste or odor changes. Water starting to taste like tap again (chlorine) means carbon is spent; a stale taste points to the post-filter.
  3. Rising TDS. The most precise check. Measure your RO water’s total dissolved solids with a TDS meter; if the reading climbs over time (toward your tap water’s level) compared with the baseline you took after installation, the membrane is losing performance.

How to Change RO Filters (Quick Version)

Filter changes are usually a short job once you’ve done one:

  1. Turn off the feed water valve and the tank valve.
  2. Open the RO faucet to release pressure.
  3. Swap the filters. Most modern systems use twist-in or quick-change cartridges (no tools). Older systems use housings you open with the included wrench — keep a towel and bucket handy.
  4. For the membrane: pull the old one from its housing and insert the new one fully.
  5. Turn the water back on, check for leaks, and flush the new filters by draining the first full tank (carbon fines and preservative rinse out).
  6. Sanitize if you’re doing a major service (see below).

The exact filter orientation and which cartridge is which varies by system — follow your manual or the diagram on the unit. The same connections and care from our installation guide apply.

Annual Sanitizing (Tank Systems)

When you change the membrane — or once a year if you want to be thorough — it’s good practice to sanitize a tank-based system. With the filters removed, add unscented household bleach in the amount your manual specifies to the first housing, reassemble without filters, run it to fill and drain the tank once or twice, then install the new filters and flush. This is meant to clear bacterial film that can develop in the tank over time. Tankless systems don’t have a storage tank, so this step is minimal or unnecessary.

What RO Maintenance Costs

Prices change, so we don’t quote them; check current prices for your system’s filters before you buy. What drives the yearly cost:

  • Pre/post filters: the most frequent purchase. Branded vs universal, and how often your water forces a change, make the biggest difference.
  • RO membrane: replaced least often, so its cost is spread over a longer interval (24 months in the iSpring RO500AK and Waterdrop X8-WAN examples above).
  • Extra stages (alkaline, UV, if equipped): add their own replacement schedule.
  • Multi-year sets: iSpring, for example, sells 6-month, 1-year, 2-year and 3-year sets for the RCC7AK; compare the per-year cost of each against your usage.

Troubleshooting Common RO Problems

Most RO issues trace back to maintenance. Here’s a quick diagnostic guide:

Symptom Likely cause Fix
No or very low water Clogged pre-filters, empty/low tank, closed valve, low pressure Check valves are open; replace pre-filters; check the tank’s air pressure against your manual
Slow tank refill Aging membrane or clogged filters; low feed pressure Replace filters; test pressure; consider a booster pump
Water tastes bad again Spent carbon filters or exhausted post-filter Replace carbon and post filters
Rising TDS / less pure Membrane wearing out Replace the membrane; verify with a TDS meter
Gurgling / dripping noise Normal for air-gap faucets; otherwise a drain-line issue Check the drain line for kinks if it’s a non-air-gap setup
White residue / cloudy water Air in new filters, or high-mineral remineralized water Usually clears after flushing a tank or two
Water won’t shut off (constant drain) Faulty check valve or shut-off valve Replace the auto shut-off valve or check valve
Leaks Tubing not fully seated, or a cracked fitting Re-seat tubing firmly; replace over-tightened fittings

If basic maintenance doesn’t resolve a problem, the membrane or a valve is often the culprit — both are replaceable parts; your manual or the maker’s support can confirm which.

FAQ

How often should I change my reverse osmosis filters?

Follow your system’s manual. As an example, iSpring’s RO500AK listing gives 6 months for the pre-filter, 12 months for the post-filter and 24 months for the RO membrane; APEC gives 6–24 months across the ROES-50’s filters. Heavy use or poor source water shortens intervals.

How long does an RO membrane last?

It depends on the system and your water; iSpring (MRO500) and Waterdrop (X8-WAN) both list 24 months for their tankless systems’ RO filters. The membrane’s lifespan depends heavily on whether you change the carbon pre-filters on time — chlorine that reaches the membrane (because the carbon is exhausted) destroys it early. Track membrane health with a TDS meter.

What happens if I don’t change my RO filters?

Flow slows as pre-filters clog, water quality and taste decline, and — most costly — chlorine breaks through the exhausted carbon and can permanently damage the membrane. The pre-filters exist to protect the membrane.

Can I use universal (generic) RO filters instead of brand-name ones?

Often yes, if your system uses standard 10-inch filter housings. Universal sets like Geekpure are made for those housings; check sizes and fittings. The exception is systems with proprietary twist-in cartridges (some Waterdrop and tankless models), which need the brand’s own filters.

How do I know if my RO membrane is going bad?

Measure your RO water’s TDS with a meter. Compare it with the reading you took after installation; if the number climbs toward your tap water’s level over time, the membrane is losing rejection and it’s time to replace it. Slower production and worse taste are secondary signs.

Do tankless RO systems need different maintenance?

The filter-change concept is the same, but tankless systems generally use the brand’s own quick-change cartridges, there’s no tank to sanitize, and some have a filter-life indicator (for example, the iSpring RO500AK’s filter-change lights, per the listing). The schedule pattern is similar: pre-filters most often, membrane least often.

Why is my reverse osmosis system producing water slowly?

The two most common causes are clogged pre-filters and low tank pressure (or an aging membrane). Replace the pre-filters if they’re overdue, check the storage tank’s air pressure against your manual, and confirm your feed pressure is within the system’s stated range (APEC’s ROES-PH75 listing, for example, gives 40–85 psi). If flow is still slow after fresh filters, the membrane may be near end of life.

Why does my RO water taste bad or different?

A return of chlorine taste means the carbon pre-filters are spent and need replacing; a stale or off taste points to an old post-filter. If the water tastes “flat,” that’s normal RO (consider a remineralization filter). Replacing the carbon and post filters on schedule prevents taste problems.

Bottom Line

Reverse osmosis maintenance is simple: change the filters on the schedule in your manual, replace the membrane when it’s due, and watch your TDS to catch a fading membrane early. Staying on schedule protects the membrane and keeps your water tasting clean. Match filters to your system (brand sets for proprietary cartridges, universal sets for standard housings), flush after every change, and your RO system will keep delivering.
