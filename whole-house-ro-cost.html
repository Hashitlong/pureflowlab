How Much Does a Whole House Reverse Osmosis System Cost in 2026?

TL;DR: There’s no honest single number for whole-house reverse osmosis, because the RO unit is only one of six or more components, and installation varies widely with your plumbing, drain and electrical access. The total is driven by (1) how much treated water your household needs, which sets the RO capacity, storage tank size and re-pressurization pump; (2) how much pre-treatment your water test says you need (sediment, carbon, iron, softening, UV); (3) installation complexity; and (4) recurring filter and membrane replacement, plus the water, sewer and electricity the system uses. Test your water, then get at least two itemized quotes from local installers. For many homes, a hybrid setup (a whole-house filter plus an under-sink RO system) covers the main goals with far less equipment.

If you’ve already decided that whole-house RO is the right choice for your situation, the next question is: what’s it actually going to cost? System prices only tell part of the story. Installation, storage tank, booster pump, pre-treatment, filter replacements, electricity and wastewater all add to it. The honest answer requires accounting for all of it.

This guide walks through every line item so you know what to budget for and what to ask installers. We don’t publish generic dollar ranges here: prices change, and local labor and plumbing conditions swing the total more than any national average can capture. Instead, use this as a checklist to build your own estimate from current prices and local quotes.

The spread is enormous because “whole-house RO” covers everything from a single commercial tankless unit DIY’d into a small home to a custom-configured setup with very large storage and professional install.

Equipment: Component by Component

A complete whole-house RO system has six required components (plus optional add-ons). Product pages usually show the RO unit and skip the rest.

The components you need

1. The RO unit (membrane + housing)

This is the line item most people price first. Common starting points:

  • Light commercial tankless (300 GPD): iSpring RCB3P. Per the listing: booster pump, 1.5:1 pure-to-drain, pressure gauge, stainless frame; residential and light commercial use; a tank is optional and not included.
  • Commercial tankless (500 GPD): iSpring RCS5T. Per the listing: 5-stage, 1.5:1, booster pump, pressure gauge.
  • Commercial tankless (1,000 GPD): iSpring CRO1000. Per the listing: 4-stage with 20″ x 4.5″ filters, 0.7 GPM, inlet and outlet gauges, with an optional PMP1000 booster pump if your pressure is below 80 psi.
  • Pre-engineered residential packages from water-treatment specialists, such as US Water Systems or Crystal Quest. Check exactly what’s included (pre-filters, tank, pump) before comparing prices.

The iSpring units are RO machines, not complete whole-house systems: you still need the storage, pump and pre-treatment below.

2. Storage tank (atmospheric)

An RO membrane produces water slowly compared with how fast a household uses it when several fixtures run at once (showers, dishwasher, laundry). You need a storage buffer. Tank size is one of the biggest equipment cost drivers, and it follows your household’s peak demand. Ask your installer to size it from your actual usage (a recent water bill helps) rather than guessing.

3. Re-pressurization pump

Water leaving an atmospheric storage tank isn’t under pressure. You need a pump (and usually a small pressure tank) to deliver normal household pressure to your fixtures. Options range from a basic jet pump with a bladder tank to variable-speed constant-pressure systems, which cost more but hold pressure steadier.

4. Pre-filtration (sediment + carbon, and more if your water needs it)

Before water reaches the RO membrane, it needs filtering. Chlorine damages the thin-film membranes most RO systems use, so carbon pre-filtration matters on city water, and sediment filtration protects the membrane from grit. Well water may also need iron removal, softening or UV. Your water test decides how far this goes, and it’s where budgets most often grow.

5. Post-treatment (remineralization, pH balancing)

RO water can be slightly acidic and taste “flat.” Many installs add:

  • An alkaline remineralization filter
  • A calcite (calcium carbonate) stage for pH correction, which can matter for metal plumbing
  • UV (optional, common on well water)

6. Drain line, fittings, valves, plumbing

The often-overlooked materials:

  • Bypass valve assembly (so you can service the system without cutting off water)
  • Pressure regulator, if needed
  • Drain line, fittings, and copper or PEX connections

Installation Costs

Installation is the biggest variable in total project cost and the easiest place to save money, if you have the skills.

DIY install

Realistic only if you’re comfortable with: - Sweating copper or making PEX expansion connections on a pressurized main water line - Installing a properly sized bypass valve assembly - Having a 120V outlet for the pump (or getting one added) - Routing a drain line for the RO wastewater, which flows whenever the membrane is producing - Sizing the system correctly for household demand - Sanitizing the storage tank on the maker’s schedule

Expect to be without water for part of the install. If you’ve never sweated copper or worked on your home’s main water line, hire a plumber for that part even if you DIY the rest. Check whether your jurisdiction requires a permit.

Professional install

A water-treatment specialist or plumber’s quote will typically include:

  • Labor (hourly or flat rate)
  • Parts and fittings
  • Permit fees, where required
  • Any drain or electrical work

Installation cost varies widely with plumbing access and pre-treatment needs, so get at least two local installer quotes and ask for them itemized.

What affects install cost the most

  • Distance from main water entry to RO location. Installs near the main line are simpler. Putting the system in a room far from where the main enters the house requires significant additional plumbing.
  • Existing drain availability. A floor drain or utility sink near the install location is critical. If a new drain line has to be run for the wastewater, the cost goes up.
  • Existing electrical. An outlet near the install location keeps things simple. Running a new circuit for the pump adds electrician time.
  • Pre-existing softener or filter. If you’re installing whole-house RO into a setup that already has a water softener or whole-house filter, the integration can be straightforward or complex depending on layout.

Ongoing Costs (Year 1 Forward)

The upfront cost isn’t the whole story. Recurring filter and membrane replacements are what surprise many homeowners.

Filter and membrane replacement

Filter Job What sets the replacement interval
Sediment pre-filter Stops sand and grit Sediment load; pressure drop across the filter
Carbon pre-filter Removes chlorine that damages the membrane Chlorine/chloramine level and gallons treated
RO membrane Removes dissolved solids Source water quality and pre-treatment; watch product-water TDS
Post-treatment carbon Final polish for taste and odor Manufacturer schedule
Alkaline remineralization (if equipped) Adds minerals back, raises pH Manufacturer schedule and gallons treated

To budget: list every cartridge and membrane in the quote, look up the current price of each replacement, and multiply by how many times per year the manufacturer says to change it. Whole-house cartridges and membranes are larger than under-sink ones, so expect this line to be well above what you’d spend on a single under-sink system. A cheap digital TDS meter (like this one, which reads 0–9,990 ppm per the listing) helps you replace the membrane when it’s actually worn rather than on guesswork.

Electricity for the pump

Pumps run when water is being used and pressure drops below the pump’s cut-in point, and some RO units also run a booster pump while producing. To estimate: take the pump’s wattage from its spec sheet, multiply by the hours it runs per day, and multiply by your utility’s rate per kWh. A variable-speed pump can use less energy than a fixed-speed pump cycling on and off.

Wastewater impact

Every RO system sends part of the incoming water to the drain. The pure-to-drain ratio tells you how much: the iSpring RCB3P’s stated 1.5:1 ratio, for example, means about 2 gallons go to the drain for every 3 gallons of RO water. Across a whole house, that adds up quickly.

To estimate your wastewater: take the treated water your household uses per day, divide by the system’s pure-to-drain ratio, and multiply by 365. Then:

  • On municipal water and sewer: multiply that volume by the water and sewer rates on your bill (utilities usually bill per 1,000 gallons or per unit). This can be a significant recurring cost, so run the numbers before you commit.
  • On a well: there’s no water bill, but the extra volume affects your well pump’s run time and your septic system’s loading. Ask your septic service whether the added flow changes your pump-out schedule, and plan the drain so the reject water doesn’t overload the system.

A higher pure-to-drain ratio is one of the best ways to keep this line down, so compare ratios when you compare systems.

Maintenance and sanitization

  • Storage tank sanitization on the maker’s schedule (DIY, or hire it out)
  • Membrane replacement labor, if you don’t do it yourself
  • Periodic water testing to confirm the system is still performing

Hidden Costs Nobody Mentions

A few things that don’t show up on product pages but show up later:

Space modification. Whole-house RO with a large storage tank needs real floor space and height. If you’re retrofitting and don’t have the space, you might need to build an enclosure, protect an unheated space from freezing, or modify electrical/drain access.

Increased water bill (municipal users). If you’re on city water with sewer billing tied to consumption, the RO wastewater raises both. Use the wastewater estimate above with your own rates.

Septic loading (well users). If you’re on a septic system, the extra water volume can affect how often the tank needs pumping. Ask your septic service.

Pump replacement. Pumps are wear items. Ask what the expected service life and warranty are, and set money aside for an eventual replacement.

Storage tank replacement. Tanks last a long time but aren’t permanent. Ask about the tank warranty.

Pre-treatment scope creep. When you start the project thinking you just need RO, you often discover your incoming water needs more than basic carbon pre-filtration: an iron filter, softener or UV. Each one is another piece of equipment with its own install and upkeep, which is why testing first matters.

How to Get an Accurate Budget

  1. Test your water first. A lab test (for example, Tap Score Advanced, which covers 116 analytes per the listing) tells you what pre-treatment you need, which is the biggest unknown in any quote.
  2. Know your demand. Pull a year of water bills (or estimate from household size and fixtures) so the RO capacity and tank can be sized correctly.
  3. Measure your pressure and note where your main enters, where the nearest drain is, and whether there’s an outlet nearby.
  4. Get at least two itemized quotes from local water-treatment specialists or plumbers. Ask them to list the RO unit, tank, pump, pre- and post-treatment, labor, permits, and anything excluded.
  5. Ask for the ongoing costs in writing: which filters and membranes, how often, and current replacement prices, plus the pure-to-drain ratio and the pump’s power draw.
  6. Ask about warranties on the RO unit, pump and tank, and who services them.
  7. Leave a contingency for surprises: plumbing problems found during install, or extra pre-treatment the water test reveals.

FAQ

How much does a whole house RO system cost installed?

It depends on capacity, storage tank size, pre-treatment and installation complexity, which is why quotes vary so much. The biggest cost drivers are storage tank size, how much pre-treatment your water needs, and how hard the install is (distance from the main, drain and electrical access). Test your water and get at least two itemized local quotes; that’s the only reliable number.

What’s the least expensive way to get whole-house RO?

Usually a DIY build around a commercial RO unit such as the iSpring RCB3P, or a pre-engineered package from a water-treatment specialist installed yourself. Either way, the RO unit is only part of the cost: you still need a storage tank, pump and pre-filtration to complete the system. Compare current prices for the full set of components, not just the RO unit.

How much does it cost to run a whole house RO system per year?

Add up four things: replacement filters (on the manufacturer’s schedule), membrane replacement spread over its service life, electricity for the pump (wattage x run time x your rate), and water and sewer charges for the wastewater (your treated-water use divided by the pure-to-drain ratio, at your utility’s rates). On metered water and sewer, wastewater can be the largest of the four.

How much does professional installation cost?

It varies with local labor rates, the plumbing modifications needed, drain availability, whether new electrical has to be run, and permit requirements. Very large storage tanks and extensive pre-treatment add time. Get at least two itemized quotes.

Is whole house RO worth the cost?

For most homeowners, probably not: a whole-house filter plus a dedicated under-sink RO system covers the most common goals (treated water at every tap for chlorine and sediment, RO water for drinking) with much less equipment and wastewater. Whole-house RO makes sense for the minority with contamination that matters at every fixture, such as high nitrates, very high TDS or brackish well water.

How long does a whole house RO system last?

With maintenance, the core system can run for many years, but it relies on replaceable parts: pre- and post-filters (most often), the RO membrane (less often, depending on water quality and pre-treatment), the pump, and eventually the storage tank. Ask your installer or the manufacturer for the expected service life and warranty of each.

How much do replacement filters cost per year?

List every cartridge and membrane in your system, look up current replacement prices, and multiply by how often the manufacturer says to change each one. Whole-house cartridges are larger than under-sink ones, and the membrane is usually the most expensive single item, so spread its cost over its expected life.

Bottom Line: What Should You Budget?

Start with a water test. Pre-treatment is the biggest unknown in any whole-house RO budget, and a test turns it into a known list.

Price the whole system, not the RO unit. Storage tank, pump, pre- and post-treatment, plumbing and installation all belong in the number, and so do five years of filters, membranes, electricity and wastewater.

Get at least two itemized local quotes. Installation cost varies widely with plumbing access and pre-treatment needs, and itemized quotes make them comparable.
