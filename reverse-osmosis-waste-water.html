Reverse Osmosis Wastewater: Is It Really 4:1? (And How to Cut It)

It’s the single most common objection to reverse osmosis: “Doesn’t it waste a ton of water?” The honest answer is yes, RO produces some wastewater — but the “4 gallons wasted for every 1 you drink” figure you’ve probably heard doesn’t describe every system. Many current systems list much better ratios, the amount is modest in household context, and there are concrete ways to cut it further. Here’s the straight story.

The Short Answer

Reverse osmosis produces a “wastewater” stream — the water that carries rejected contaminants down the drain. How much depends on the system:

  • Conventional systems without a pump: can send several gallons to drain per gallon of pure water, depending on design and water pressure. This is where RO’s wasteful reputation comes from. Some still do — the Express Water Countertop RO, for example, lists 1:3 pure-to-drain.
  • Many current tankless systems: list 1:1 to 3:1 pure-to-drain — for example, 1:1 for the Waterdrop G2, 2:1 for the G3P600 and iSpring RO500AK, and 3:1 for the Waterdrop G3P800, per their listings.
  • Permeate-pump systems: APEC says its RO-PERM produces up to 80% less wastewater, per the listing.

In context, most households use RO only for drinking and cooking, so the wastewater is usually small next to what the home already sends down the drain from toilets, showers, and laundry. It’s a real consideration — not the dealbreaker it’s often made out to be.

What “Wastewater” Actually Is

To purify water, the RO membrane separates it into two streams: the permeate (your clean drinking water) and the concentrate or reject (water carrying the rejected dissolved solids and contaminants, sent to the drain). That reject stream is what’s called wastewater.

It’s worth being precise about what it is and isn’t. RO wastewater is not sewage — it’s just your tap water with a higher concentration of the minerals and contaminants the membrane removed. It’s flushed continuously to keep the membrane from fouling. Calling it “wasted” is fair in that it goes down the drain, but some of it can be reused for non-drinking purposes, with care (more below).

How Much Water Does RO Really Waste?

Context matters here. A typical household uses RO only for drinking and cooking. To estimate your own drain water:

  • Estimate how much RO water your household drinks and cooks with per day.
  • Apply your system’s listed ratio: at 2:1 pure-to-drain, drain water is half the pure water; at 1:3, it’s three times the pure water.
  • Compare that with the rest of your household water use (toilets, showers, laundry) from your water bill.

For many homes RO wastewater is a small share of total water use. It’s a more meaningful concern if you’re on a well (you pay to pump every gallon), in a drought/water-restricted area, or running RO at high volume — in which case the efficiency upgrades below matter more.

3. Keep your water pressure in range. RO efficiency depends on adequate feed pressure; check the range in your system’s manual (APEC’s ROES-PH75 listing, for example, gives 40–85 psi). Low pressure generally increases waste, so a booster pump or permeate pump on low-pressure homes can help.

4. Maintain the system. Clogged pre-filters and an aging membrane both worsen the ratio. Staying on your maintenance schedule keeps efficiency where it should be.

5. Reuse the reject water (with care). RO wastewater is concentrated tap water — not sewage — so some people collect it for non-drinking uses: watering ornamental plants, cleaning, or flushing. Because it carries whatever the membrane removed at higher concentration, don’t drink it, and be cautious using it on edible plants. It takes a little plumbing or a collection container, and you should avoid it for salt-sensitive plants, but it’s a legitimate way to make the “waste” useful.

How to Calculate Your System’s Ratio

Want to know your actual pure-to-drain ratio? It’s a simple measurement:

  1. Disconnect the two output lines temporarily — the line going to your tank/faucet (pure) and the line going to the drain (reject) — and run each into a separate measuring container.
  2. Run the system for a set time (say, a few minutes) and measure how much collects in each container.
  3. Divide. If you collected 2 cups of pure water and 4 cups of drain water, your ratio is 1:2 (pure:drain). If you got 2 cups pure and 2 cups drain, you’re at 1:1.

If your measured waste is much higher than the manufacturer’s spec, the usual culprits are low feed pressure, a clogged flow restrictor, or an aging membrane — all fixable. Restoring pressure (or adding a booster/permeate pump) and staying current on maintenance keeps the ratio where it should be.

FAQ

How much water does a reverse osmosis system waste?

It depends on the system, so check the listed pure-to-drain ratio. Examples from current listings: 1:3 for the Express Water Countertop RO, 1:1 for the Waterdrop G2, 2:1 for the G3P600, 3:1 for the G3P800; APEC says its permeate-pump RO-PERM produces up to 80% less wastewater. In household terms, RO is usually used only for drinking and cooking, so the drain water is typically small next to toilets, showers, and laundry.

Is reverse osmosis bad for the environment?

It produces some wastewater, but in context it’s a small share of household use, and it avoids the plastic waste and transport footprint of bottled water. Choosing a tankless or permeate-pump system minimizes the waste, and the reject water can be reused for non-drinking purposes. Compared with bottled water, RO avoids bottles and shipping.

Can I reuse reverse osmosis wastewater?

Yes, with care. RO reject water is concentrated tap water (not sewage), so it can be collected for watering ornamental plants, cleaning, or flushing toilets. Because it carries what the membrane removed at a higher concentration, don’t drink it, and be cautious with salt-sensitive or edible plants.

What is the most water-efficient reverse osmosis system?

Compare listed ratios. Among the systems we cover, the Waterdrop G3P800 and X16 list 3:1 pure-to-drain and the Waterdrop G3P600 lists 2:1; among countertop units, the SimPure Y7P-BW lists 4:1. For a tank system, the APEC Ultimate RO-PERM uses a permeate pump that APEC says cuts wastewater by up to 80%.

Why does reverse osmosis need to waste any water at all?

The reject stream continuously flushes the contaminants the membrane removes off its surface — without it, the membrane would foul. So some wastewater is inherent to how RO works; the goal isn’t to eliminate it but to minimize it with efficient design.

Does reverse osmosis wastewater go down the sewer?

Yes, in a standard install the reject water connects to your sink’s drain line and goes to the sewer (or septic) like any other drain water. It’s concentrated tap water, so it goes to the drain like other kitchen water. If you’d rather not send it to the drain, some people reroute it to a collection container to reuse for plants or cleaning.

Will a permeate pump work on my existing RO system?

In many cases, yes — a permeate pump can be added to compatible standard tank-based RO systems to cut wastewater and boost output, without electricity. Compatibility depends on your system’s design, so check before buying. If you’re shopping new and efficiency is a priority, a system built around one (like the APEC RO-PERM) is the simplest route.

Bottom Line

Reverse osmosis does produce wastewater — but the “4:1” reputation doesn’t fit every system. Many tankless systems list 2:1 or 3:1 pure-to-drain, APEC says its permeate-pump RO-PERM cuts waste up to 80%, and in household terms the amount is usually small next to everyday water use. If efficiency matters to you — especially on a well or in a water-restricted area — choose a tankless or permeate-pump system, keep your pressure and maintenance up, and reuse the reject water where practical. It’s a real factor, not a reason to skip RO.
