What to Look For in a Reverse Osmosis System

If you’re comparing systems beyond this list, here’s the actual decision framework.

Stages and What They Do

Most under-sink RO systems are described as “5-stage” or “8-stage.” The number refers to filter cartridges in series, not necessarily quality. The standard stages:

  1. Sediment filter — catches sand, rust, scale before they reach the membrane
  2. Carbon block — removes chlorine (which destroys the RO membrane), taste, odor
  3. Carbon polish — finer carbon for additional taste/chemical removal
  4. RO membrane — the actual reverse osmosis step (the heart of the system)
  5. Post-carbon — final polish before the faucet
  6. Optional: alkaline / remineralization — adds minerals back after RO
  7. Optional: UV sterilization — kills bacteria (most useful for well water)
  8. Optional: TDS reduction stage — extra polish for very high TDS sources

More stages isn’t automatically better. What matters is that the stages that are present are the right ones for your water. For city water, 5 stages is plenty. For well water with bacteria concerns, UV is worth adding. For people who don’t like flat-tasting RO water, alkaline remineralization solves it.

Tankless vs Tank-Based

Tankless RO systems (like the Waterdrop G3P800 and G3P600) produce water on demand. Pros: no bulky storage tank, no tank to sanitize, much higher rated output. Cons: usually cost more upfront and need an electrical outlet under the sink.

Tank-based RO systems (like the APEC ROES-50X, Express Water 5-Stage RO, iSpring RCC7AK) store treated water in a pressurized tank under the sink. Pros: usually cost less upfront, no electrical required. Cons: storage tank takes cabinet space, the tank needs periodic sanitizing, slower refill if you use a lot of water.

If cabinet space is tight, tankless is usually the deciding factor.

GPD Ratings

GPD (gallons per day) is the rated production capacity of the RO membrane. For under-sink drinking-water-only use:

  • 50 GPD: Fine for 1-2 person households, modest water use
  • 75-100 GPD: Good for 3-4 person households
  • 400-600 GPD: Tankless systems, basically unlimited for any household
  • 800 GPD: Premium tankless, complete overkill for residential drinking water

Higher GPD mainly buys speed. It doesn’t tell you how well a system removes a given contaminant; the certification list does that.

Wastewater Ratio

RO systems produce both treated water and wastewater (water rejected by the membrane). The ratio is expressed as “X:1 pure-to-drain” — so a 2:1 system makes 2 gallons of pure water for every gallon sent down the drain.

Older tank-based designs often send more water to drain than they produce, and many basic listings don’t state a ratio at all. Newer tankless systems list 2:1 or 3:1: the Waterdrop G3P600 is 2:1 and the G3P800 is 3:1, per their listings.

Wastewater ratio matters if you’re on a well or have water billing — over a year, a more efficient system can save a lot of water. For most municipal water users it’s a minor consideration.

NSF Certifications

The certifications that actually matter for an RO system:

  • NSF/ANSI 42 — chlorine, taste, odor reduction
  • NSF/ANSI 53 — health-related contaminants (lead, VOCs, cysts, heavy metals)
  • NSF/ANSI 58 — RO systems: TDS reduction, plus optional claims such as fluoride or arsenic
  • NSF/ANSI 372 — lead-free materials throughout the system

Check which standards a listing actually names, and who certified the product. “NSF certified” with no standard named tells you little. Among our picks, the G3P800 and AquaTru Classic list the most standards; the G3P600 and iSpring RCC7AK list NSF/ANSI 58; the Express Water kit says “NSF Certified” without naming a standard; and the APEC ROES-50X listing makes no NSF claim.

Faucet Style

A surprisingly important detail. RO systems include a dedicated faucet for the treated water (separate from your main sink faucet). Older systems use a basic chrome lever; newer systems offer brushed nickel, matte black, and the Waterdrop “smart” faucets with built-in LED status indicators and TDS displays.

Faucet style matters because it’s the most visible part of the install and the only thing your guests will see. If your kitchen is going to be photographed for real estate purposes, the upgraded faucet styles are worth it.

What an RO System Actually Costs

The sticker price is just the start. Prices change often, so check each listing for the current figure, then think about the cost of ownership:

System type: Tankless units and countertop units usually cost more upfront than basic tank-based 5-stage systems. Extra stages (alkaline, UV) and smart faucets add to the price.

Installation: Under-sink systems are DIY-friendly if you’re moderately handy. If you hire a plumber, get at least two quotes; cost varies with plumbing access and whether you need a hole drilled in stone. Countertop units need no installation.

Filter replacements: This is the recurring cost. Check the replacement schedule and filter part numbers before you buy. For example, iSpring sells RCC7AK sets on 6-month (F4AK), 1-year (F9K), 2-year (F19K75) and 3-year (F28K75) schedules, and AquaTru says its Classic filters last 600–1,200 gallons (6 months to 2 years). Price the replacement set on Amazon and multiply by how many you’ll need over five years.

Membrane replacement: The RO membrane lasts longer than the pre-filters, but it will eventually need replacing too. It’s usually the most expensive single cartridge.

Installation Difficulty (Honest Take)

Under-sink RO installation is genuinely DIY-friendly if you’re moderately handy. The process:

  1. Shut off cold water supply at the angle stop under the sink
  2. Install a T-fitting on the cold water line for the RO feed
  3. Drill a hole in your sink (or use an existing sprayer hole) for the RO faucet
  4. Drill a small hole in the drain line for the wastewater connection (using a kit-provided saddle clamp)
  5. Mount the filter housing to the cabinet wall
  6. Connect tubing (tankless systems also need a 110V outlet)
  7. Flush the system for 1-2 hours before drinking

Total time: 1-2 hours for a first install. Stainless steel sinks need a stepped drill bit for the faucet hole. Granite/quartz countertops require a diamond hole saw — at that point, consider paying a plumber.

Common DIY mistakes: forgetting to flush the system before drinking (initial water tastes terrible), under-tightening fittings (slow leaks), and installing the drain saddle clamp on the wrong section of drain pipe (above the trap, not below).

Maintenance and Filter Replacement

Component Replacement Interval Why It Matters
Sediment filter 6-12 months Catches sediment before it reaches the membrane
Carbon pre-filter 6-12 months Removes chlorine, which damages RO membranes
Carbon post-filter 12 months Final polish before the faucet
RO membrane 2-5 years Does the actual RO work; usually the priciest cartridge
Alkaline cartridge (if applicable) 12 months Only on systems with a remineralization stage
System sanitization Annually Mainly for tank-based systems

Set a recurring calendar reminder for filter changes, and follow the maker’s schedule for your model. The pre-filters protect the RO membrane; neglect them and the membrane wears out much sooner.

Many tankless systems use quick-change cartridges that twist or push in; Waterdrop and iSpring both describe filter swaps that need no tools. Traditional tank-based designs use screw-on housings, so you shut off the water and unscrew each housing to change filters.

FAQ

Is reverse osmosis water safe to drink?

RO is a widely used drinking-water treatment. The main concern critics raise is mineral content: RO removes calcium, magnesium, and other minerals along with contaminants. If that concerns you, get a system with a remineralization stage (like the iSpring RCC7AK) that adds minerals back after the RO membrane. We cover the question in more depth in Is Reverse Osmosis Water Good for You?

How long does an RO membrane last?

It depends on your water and on keeping the pre-filters current; follow the maker’s schedule for your model (iSpring’s RO500AK membrane, for example, is rated for 24 months). Chlorine damages RO membranes, so if you’re on city water and the carbon pre-filters are spent, the membrane wears out early.

Does RO remove fluoride?

Yes. Fluoride is one of the dissolved ions an RO membrane rejects. If fluoride is your main concern, check whether the specific system’s certification includes a fluoride-reduction claim. See our fluoride guide.

Does RO remove PFAS / “forever chemicals”?

RO can reduce PFAS, but don’t assume every RO system is certified for it. An NSF/ANSI 58 certification alone doesn’t mean a system was certified for PFOA/PFOS reduction; look for PFOA/PFOS in the certified-contaminant list. Of our picks, the AquaTru Classic’s listing names PFOA/PFOS among its 84 contaminants certified by IAPMO.

What’s the difference between a tankless and tank-based RO system?

Tankless systems produce water on demand and skip the storage tank entirely: saves cabinet space, no tank to sanitize, much higher rated output. Tank-based systems store treated water in a pressurized tank: usually cheaper upfront and no electrical needed. Both work; the choice comes down to budget and how much you value cabinet space.

Can I install an RO system myself?

Yes, if you’re moderately handy. Plan 1-2 hours for a first install. The trickiest part is drilling a hole in your sink for the dedicated RO faucet — stainless sinks need a stepped drill bit, granite/quartz countertops need a diamond hole saw and consider paying a plumber instead.

Do RO systems waste a lot of water?

Older tank-based designs often send more water to drain than they produce. Newer tankless systems list much better ratios: 2:1 for the Waterdrop G3P600 and 3:1 for the G3P800, per their listings. If wastewater is a concern, lean toward tankless and check the listed ratio.

Will RO work with low water pressure?

Tank-based RO needs enough feed pressure to work well; APEC, for example, lists 40–85 psi for its ROES-PH75. Below that, output drops. Options are a system with a permeate pump (the APEC RO-PERM listing says its pump fills the tank 2–3x faster and cuts wastewater by up to 80%) or one with a booster pump. Tankless units like the Waterdrop G3P600 run on electricity (its listing says it needs an outlet under the sink); check the listing’s pressure range before buying.
