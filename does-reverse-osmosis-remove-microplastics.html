Does Reverse Osmosis Remove Microplastics? (Yes — Here's What the Science Says)

Yes — reverse osmosis removes microplastics, and it’s one of the more effective home methods for doing so. The RO membrane is far finer than microplastic particles, so it acts as a physical barrier to them, and it is also expected to reject much of the smaller nanoplastic fraction that can pass ordinary filters. This guide explains how RO removes microplastics, what is and isn’t known, and why the pitcher filter on your counter can’t do the same job.

The Short Answer

Reverse osmosis is one of the stronger home options against microplastics because of scale: the RO membrane is designed to reject dissolved salts, which are far smaller than any plastic particle. Microplastics are commonly defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 millimeters, so even very small ones are much larger than what the membrane is built to stop. They are held back and flushed away with the wastewater.

Nanoplastics — the much smaller fragments (under 1 micron) that newer research methods can detect — are also far larger than dissolved ions, so an intact membrane should reject much of them. No home filter is perfect, and seals, O-rings or a damaged membrane can let particles bypass, so maintenance matters.

How Reverse Osmosis Removes Microplastics

Reverse osmosis works by forcing water under pressure through a semipermeable membrane so fine it rejects dissolved ions — things far smaller than any plastic particle. (For the full mechanism, see What Is Reverse Osmosis Water?.) Because microplastics are much larger than the ions the membrane is designed to reject, they’re physically blocked. It’s a size barrier rather than adsorption, which is why RO is well suited to particles.

This is fundamentally different from how a carbon filter works. Carbon removes contaminants by adsorption (molecules sticking to the carbon surface), which is great for chlorine and taste but doesn’t reliably catch plastic particles. A membrane that physically strains the water is the right tool for the job.

What the Science Actually Found

Microplastics in drinking water have drawn growing attention in recent years. What’s reasonably well established:

  • Microplastics have been detected in both tap water and bottled water.
  • Newer imaging methods can detect much smaller particles, including nanoplastics, than earlier methods could, so reported particle counts have gone up as the methods improved.
  • The health effects of ingesting micro- and nanoplastics are still being researched and aren’t established. The World Health Organization has reviewed microplastics in drinking water and called for more research.

The practical takeaway: bottled water is not a reliable way to avoid plastics, and most carbon pitcher filters aren’t designed to remove them. Reverse osmosis is one of the few approaches that addresses both microplastics and the smaller nanoplastics, because it filters at the molecular scale.

These are general ratings based on how each method works, not measured percentages. Reverse osmosis and distillation are the two that act on the smallest particles, and RO is the more practical for daily home use.

Which Filters Remove Microplastics — and Which Don’t

If reducing plastic particles is a priority, the filtration type matters more than the brand:

  • Reverse osmosis: a physical barrier to micro- and nanoplastics. Any working RO membrane does this — see our best reverse osmosis systems guide or, for renters, the countertop options.
  • Distillation: very effective, but impractical for most households’ daily volume.
  • Tight carbon block filters: can capture larger microplastics, but won’t reliably stop nanoplastics.
  • Standard pitcher and faucet filters: built for chlorine and taste, not particle removal — don’t rely on them for plastics.
  • Refrigerator filters: same limitation as pitcher filters.

If you want a certification-backed claim rather than a general one, look for microplastics reduction under NSF/ANSI 401. Can You Test Your Water for Microplastics at Home?

Honestly, not really — and it’s worth being straight about this. Detecting and counting microplastics (let alone nanoplastics) requires specialized lab equipment like spectroscopy; the home water test strips and kits that measure things like lead, hardness, and chlorine cannot measure plastic particles. A few specialty labs offer microplastic analysis as a paid service, but it isn’t a routine home test.

The practical move isn’t to test for plastics — it’s to filter them out preemptively with reverse osmosis, since the method works regardless of how many particles your specific water contains. You can confirm your RO system is healthy overall with a simple TDS meter (it verifies the membrane is reducing dissolved solids), even though TDS doesn’t measure plastics directly.

Where Microplastics in Water Come From

Understanding the sources helps explain why they’re so hard to avoid without filtration:

  • Plastic packaging and bottles. Bottled water can pick up particles from the bottle and cap.
  • Degraded plastic waste. Larger plastic litter breaks down in the environment into ever-smaller fragments that reach water supplies.
  • Water distribution pipes. Plastic piping and fittings between the treatment plant and your tap may shed particles.
  • Synthetic textiles. Washing machines release microfibers from polyester and nylon clothing into wastewater, some of which cycles back.
  • Industrial and consumer products. Microbeads (now banned in many products), tire wear, and manufacturing all contribute.

Because the sources are everywhere, both municipal and well water can carry microplastics. Conventional municipal treatment removes many larger particles but isn’t specifically designed for the smallest ones, which is why some people add point-of-use reverse osmosis.

FAQ

Does reverse osmosis remove nanoplastics too?

Largely, based on how the membrane works. Nanoplastics (particles under 1 micron) are smaller than microplastics but still much larger than the dissolved ions an RO membrane is built to reject, so an intact membrane should hold most of them back. Few other home methods act on particles that small.

Does a Brita or pitcher filter remove microplastics?

Not reliably. Standard pitcher, faucet, and refrigerator filters use activated carbon designed for chlorine and taste, not particle filtration. They may capture some larger particles incidentally, but they’re not a dependable barrier against microplastics and don’t stop nanoplastics. Reverse osmosis is the practical home method.

Is bottled water free of microplastics?

No. Microplastics have been found in bottled water as well as tap water, and the bottle and cap are themselves plastic. Switching to bottled water is not a reliable way to avoid plastics.

Does boiling water remove microplastics?

Not reliably. Some research suggests boiling very hard water can trap a portion of particles inside the limescale that forms, but it’s inconsistent and doesn’t work for soft water. For dependable removal, you need reverse osmosis or distillation, not boiling.

How do I know my RO system is removing microplastics?

You can’t measure plastics at home. RO acts on them because of the membrane’s fineness, so the practical check is that the system is working properly overall. Confirm the system is healthy overall with a TDS meter (it shows the membrane is reducing dissolved solids) and keep up with filter changes.

Do I need a special filter for microplastics, or does a normal RO system work?

A normal reverse osmosis membrane already acts on microplastics — you don’t need a special add-on. If you want a certified claim, look for NSF/ANSI 401 microplastics reduction on the system’s certification. Otherwise, choose an RO system from our best RO guide and keep it maintained.

Are there microplastics in tap water?

Yes — studies have detected microplastics in tap water. The amount varies by location and source, and there is no routine home test for it. Reverse osmosis acts on them regardless of how many your specific water contains.

Are microplastics in drinking water harmful?

The health effects of ingesting micro- and nanoplastics are still being actively researched and aren’t fully established. There’s reasonable precautionary interest in reducing exposure, which is why many people choose to filter them out — but current science hasn’t quantified the risk. We stick to what’s known: RO is a physical barrier to them if you want to reduce your exposure.

Bottom Line

Reverse osmosis removes microplastics, and is expected to hold back much of the smaller nanoplastic fraction too, because its membrane is far finer than any plastic particle. Carbon pitcher filters, fridge filters, and bottled water don’t offer the same barrier. If reducing your exposure to plastic particles is a goal, a well-maintained RO system is a practical choice, and an NSF/ANSI 401 microplastics claim is the certification to look for.
